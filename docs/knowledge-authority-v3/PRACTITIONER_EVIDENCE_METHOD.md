# Practitioner evidence method

Date: 21 October 2026

## Why this layer exists

Government, standards, industry and manufacturer material are strong sources for requirements, building science and product constraints. They do not usually answer the practical homeowner question: **what proportionate intervention is plausible on this roof - investigate, repair, restore or replace?**

RoofHub therefore keeps a separate practitioner-evidence layer for useful observations published by New Zealand roofing businesses. It is deliberately not treated as the same class of evidence as regulation, BRANZ research, RANZ/MRM guidance or manufacturer technical literature.

The objective is not to collect contractor opinions. It is to extract **specific observations about a condition, intervention and result**, record the source's commercial interest, corroborate what can be corroborated, and then write an independent RoofHub synthesis.

## Evidence classes

1. **Regulatory / government** - legal requirements, consent/exemption guidance, safety and consumer rules.
2. **Building science / industry guidance** - mechanisms, accepted practice and industry guidance.
3. **Manufacturer technical** - product-specific dimensions, minimum pitches/falls, compatibility, environment, maintenance, warranty and installation constraints.
4. **Practitioner observation** - published contractor case studies, project records and sufficiently specific field guidance.
5. **Market observation** - public pricing or scope observations, kept separate from estimator rates.
6. **RoofHub synthesis** - the independent conclusion after comparing the above.

A manufacturer is authoritative about its own system constraints; it is not automatically authoritative about which competing product is best. A contractor can be highly informative about a real project; a contractor's service recommendation remains commercially interested.

## How practitioner sources are selected

Useful practitioner material scores well on several of these signals:

- **Project specificity:** a real roof or clearly described condition rather than generic sales copy.
- **Problem depth:** what was actually wrong, not merely “old roof”.
- **Scope detail:** what was repaired, restored, replaced or investigated.
- **Decision value:** it helps distinguish two or more plausible pathways.
- **Photographic/project evidence:** before/after/project imagery or specific job notes can strengthen usefulness, without proving every claim independently.
- **NZ relevance:** New Zealand roof systems, climate, practice and terminology.
- **Source clarity:** identifiable business, stable page and enough detail to understand what is being claimed.
- **Commercial-bias transparency:** RoofHub can identify whether the source sells repair, restoration, replacement or multiple services.
- **Corroboration:** relevant government, industry, building-science or manufacturer material supports the mechanism or constraint.
- **Counterexamples:** sources that show a legitimate alternative are particularly valuable because they reduce sales-first conclusions.

### Search visibility is a discovery signal, not traffic proof

RoofHub may use repeated appearance in high-intent NZ search results to discover useful practitioner material. That indicates that search engines are surfacing the page for the intent. **It does not prove the page receives a particular amount of traffic, conversion or user engagement.** RoofHub does not have competitors' private analytics and should never state otherwise.

Domain age can be a secondary trust signal but is not a substitute for depth or evidence quality.

## What receives low weight or is rejected

RoofHub does not elevate a claim merely because several businesses repeat it. Low-weight or rejected material includes:

- thin generic SEO/service pages with no meaningful diagnostic detail;
- testimonials used as technical evidence;
- unsupported universal lifespan, price or percentage claims;
- numeric “replace after X years” rules without system/condition context;
- blanket “restoration always saves money” or “replacement is always better” claims;
- manufacturer marketing presented as independent comparison;
- copied or lightly paraphrased competitor text;
- ambiguous or unverifiable project claims that cannot be bounded appropriately.

Where a useful page also contains promotional claims, RoofHub can extract only the bounded observation and explicitly exclude the promotional inference.

## Claim-to-synthesis workflow

For a high-intent problem:

1. Define the decision, not just the keyword.
2. Study useful NZ search results and identify distinct solution pathways.
3. Extract narrowly bounded practitioner observations into `PractitionerObservation` records.
4. Record source type, project specificity, region, commercial interest, confidence and limitations.
5. Check technical/safety/product constraints against primary or industry sources.
6. Seek counterexamples where possible.
7. Identify genuine conflicts or uncertainty rather than forcing consensus.
8. Write the RoofHub synthesis from scratch.
9. Publish the synthesis as a visitor resource and make the same structured content retrievable by Ask RoofHub.
10. Preserve source links so visitors can inspect the underlying material.

The public writing should not mirror the source's structure or wording. The source establishes evidence for a bounded point; RoofHub creates its own explanation and decision framework.

## Neutrality rule: lowest adequate intervention

RoofHub should not default to the most comprehensive or expensive intervention. The governing question is:

> What is the lowest adequate intervention that is plausibly capable of solving the user's actual roof problem without disguising a broader failure?

That may be investigation, a local repair, restoration, replacement of selected components, or full reroofing. The decision depends on spread, recurrence, visible failure mode, safety concerns, system compatibility and hidden-condition uncertainty - **not roof age alone and not the service a source happens to sell.**

## Remote evidence limits

User-supplied photos can later support directional triage, provided the user can take them safely. They cannot prove hidden underlay, timber/substrate condition, fixing condition, remaining coating thickness or watertightness throughout a roof.

Satellite/aerial/Google Earth-style imagery can be useful for rough geometry, roof form, visible additions and access/site context. It must **not** be presented as reliable evidence of corrosion depth, fixings, underlay, hidden timber or a watertightness diagnosis.

No RoofHub flow should ask a user to climb onto a roof to improve an AI assessment.
